Research

Job Market Paper

  • Graduates in a Cycle: The Effect of Business Cycle Trajectories on Labor Market Outcomes of College Graduates — PDF

    Abstract: I re-evaluate the long-term effects of graduating from college during a recession by focusing on entire business cycle trajectories, as opposed to unemployment at graduation. Using CPS data from 1976-2024, I suggest that the persistent gap in earnings between those graduating during high versus low unemployment chiefly corresponds to the effects of unemployment at graduation on graduates of lower GPA, SAT scores and socio-economic background who enrolled in lower-tier colleges because unemployment was high at the time of their college enrollment. While scarring effects of graduating during a recession are large and last for over a decade for these marginal graduates, the scarring effects are smaller than previously thought and fade within three years for student cohorts enrolling in college in a low-unemployment labor market. I also show that adverse labor market conditions beyond the year of college graduation exert a stronger influence on long-term outcomes of graduates than the unemployment rate at graduation. Following unemployment trajectories and accounting for selective enrollment highlights the policy-relevant vulnerability of marginal graduates to recessions.

    Working Papers

    • Competition and the Gender Pay Gap: Evidence from Russian Trade Withdrawal — PDF

      Abstract: In 2014, Russia imposed an import embargo on selected goods, effectively reducing trade flows and decreasing import competition across several industries. In this paper, I analyze the effect of this decrease in product market competition on the gender pay gap in Russia. This research complements the body of evidence which implies that increases in product market competition lead to lower gender pay gaps. The empirical analysis relates 2011-2019 industry-specific gender wage gaps estimated from the Russian Longitudinal Monitoring Survey to the industry-level evolution of import penetration. The results show that a 10 percentage point reduction in import competition leads to an increase of about 4 percentage points in the gender pay gap. This increase is smaller in magnitude than the effect found in the US but larger than that observed in some Eastern European countries. This phenomenon partly corresponds to an exacerbation of the gender employment gap driven primarily by high-skilled women leaving industries where import shares, and consequently competition, declined.

    • “(Un)Lucky Women: The Effect of Business Cycle Trajectories on Female College Graduates” — new draft coming soon!

      I study how business cycle conditions at college enrollment and graduation jointly shape the outcomes of college-educated women. I show that women who enter college during periods of high unemployment invest more in education, both in terms of study time and field of study. These women also respond differently to adverse labor market conditions at graduation. While women who enrolled during good times reduce labor force participation and working hours when graduating into a weak labor market, women who enrolled during bad times remain more attached to the labor force and increase hours worked. At the same time, they experience larger wage losses and reduced fertility. These patterns suggest that the effects of graduating during a recession depend on the economic conditions experienced several years earlier. For women, business cycle conditions at college entry affect not only selection into college, but also subsequent educational investment and the margins through which they respond to adverse conditions at labor market entry.

    Work in Progress

    • Sources of Upper-Tail Human Capital: Evidence from the Prussian Education Reforms in the Early 19th Century

      with Konstantin Bakharev, Roman Dimakov, and Vladimir Shchukin

      How can nations lead in science and technology? Education boosts human capital, crucial for economic development. This study examines whether large-scale education reforms, exemplified by the Prussia’s early 19th century reforms, can cultivate the very upper-tail human capital -- leading academics. After its defeat by Napoleon, Prussia reformed its education system, emphasizing natural sciences and technology. We compare Prussian cities affected by these reforms with unaffected German-speaking cities, using a Diff-in-Diffs approach for 5-year birth cohorts over 1730-1855. Our measure of upper-tail human capital is the number of prominent scientists born in each city. Results show that after the reforms, Prussian cities produced from 36 to more than 100 scientists in 50 years. We contribute to research on human capital origins and the study of prominent individuals, offering the first systematic analysis of national education reforms in Prussia and their association with advanced skill formation. Our findings suggest positive association between scientific leadership and high-level talent.
